WRITING · SHORT STORY

The God Who Watches

By MD MAZIDUL ISLAM · 1st Sem, Dept. Of Statistics PUC

"Either one is wickeder than animal, or nobler than God.”

The words left Damaris the way breath leaves a dying man — soft, not even meant to be heard. As if silence, for once, was not enough to hold what sat inside him.

Outside the window of Dr. Mensah's study, a man was running across the field behind the clinic, arms full of a shaking gazelle — the jungle had almost swallowed. The man's face was cut from hurry itself, every step a small fight against time running out. Damaris watched him the way one watches a memory instead of a moment. His eyes did not blink. His hands did not move. As if he has died inside while living outside.

Silence and More Silence.

The door opened the way storms open — no warning, only weight. Dr. Mensah walked in mid-thought, coat half off one shoulder, the smell of old books and older certainty trailing behind him like a second self. He was a man built from years of listening to other people's pain and calling it a job.

"Hey — what's up, man?" he said, dropping into the chair. As though the question were light, as though the room already know better.

"I wish I could've saved her."

"Hey, Hey — stop that." Mensah leaned forward, his voice caught somewhere between comfort and command. "Stop talking to yourself like that. It wasn't on you."

"I wish I could've saved her," Damaris said again, quieter, as if saying it twice might drag her back into the present.

"Stop it." Mensah's voice sharpened at the edges. "It was her fate. Now stop this." A pause, sharp and pointed.

"Your aunty called me, you know. You haven't been going to church."

Damaris turned his head back to the window, and inside that small motion lived an entire winter. What the mind won't say out loud, the memory says in pictures.

It began with a road, empty and dark, and a car ahead of him moving the way something moves when it is running from its own shadow. A hand pressed flat against the inside of the back window, fingers spread wide like too far from shore. Then her scream. It did not sound like one scream. It sounded like many small ones stitched together, each one saying something different — the first one said help, the next one said please, the one after that said God, over and over, as if saying it enough times might keep her inside her own body. And then the screaming thinned, and thinned again, until it was barely a sound at all — more like a held breath that never got to leave.

Damaris kicked his motorcycle to life and rode after the car with everything he had. His hands shook on the handles, not from cold but from the kind of fear that lives in the chest and moves down into the arms. The road bent and dropped and rose again, and every turn he took too fast, seemed too slow. She screamed trying to stop, His engine screamed trying to reach her, and between both screams, Damaris became voiceless. Rocks kicked up under his tires. Wind tore at his eyes until they watered, and still he did not slow down, because slowing down felt like giving up, and giving up felt like killing her with his own hands.

Five kilometers passed like this — his whole body bent forward, chasing a red light that kept shrinking into the night, kept turning corners just before he did, kept staying just barely out of reach, the way hope always stays just barely out of reach when you need it the most. He shouted into the wind, knowing it would not reach anyone, knowing it did not matter, saying it anyway because his mind needed something apart from fear.

Then the road turned once more, and his front wheel caught a stone he never saw, and the ground came up to meet him hard and sudden and without any warning at all.

He did not know how long he lay there. When the world came back to him, the car — and everything it had carried away — was gone. Not stopped somewhere ahead. Just gone, the way bad things go when no one is watching closely enough to stop them.

"Okay — okay." Mensah's hands went up, calming, the way a person calms an animal that might still bite. "That is not on you. Everything happens for a reason — you know this. 'For My thoughts are not your thoughts, and My ways are not your ways.’ He said it the way men say things they have said too many times to still feel them. "You are not to blame for someone else's sin. And your aunty — she says all this sadness, this worry, it's because you have walked away from your God."

Damaris's voice, when it came, was barely a shape. "Which God?"

"The God who is with you always." Something thin and impatient crossed Mensah's face, the way a teacher looks at a student who should already know the answer. "Everywhere. All-powerful. All-knowing."

A silence. Then: "All-knowing." Damaris turned the word over slowly, like a stone with something small and dark crawling under it. "Did He know — that some men would use a girl the way animals use whatever they can catch?"

"Yes." Mensah's jaw tightened now, his patience thinning at the edges. "He knows everything. And the men who did it — they will stand in front of Him on the Day of Judgment."

"So, He knew." Damaris's eyes had not left the window, though the man and the animal were long gone from it by now. "And He could have stopped it — you said He's all-powerful, didn't you? So why didn't He? Are you telling me to bow down to someone who sits up there with all that power and just watches while an innocent girl's body gets torn apart like old cloth?"

Something in the room's air changed, the way air changes right before a storm breaks.

"You have to understand — it's not that simple." Mensah's words came faster now, sharp, a man guarding a wall he had built his whole life on top of. "This life is only the test. There is something after. There is judgment. You cannot pull one page out of the whole book and call it the ending. You need to repent for talking like this." His hands moved with each word now, like he was trying to shape the air into something solid enough to hold him up. "God's ways are not our ways. If He stopped every bad thing from happening, how would He ever test the wicked? If the whole world was only good, with no darkness anywhere in it — how would goodness ever prove itself?"

Damaris let the question sit in the air like smoke that refuses to move. Then, in a voice worn thin, he kept going.

"When I fell off the bike," he said, "the car disappeared into the dark, the same way guilt disappears into men who were never taught to carry it. And when I got up and ran again — five kilometers further down that same road — I found her."

Damaris in a murmuring voice…

"She was left there. Soaked through red, like the ground itself had opened up just to hold her. They had used her the way stray dogs use whatever they find already fallen — taken her apart, limbs by limbs, and walked away, until there was nothing left that even a mother could still call her daughter's face."

He looked, then, not at Mensah but into him — the way a person looks down a well to see if it still holds water, or only holds the memory of once being full.

"What if that blood had worn your sister's name?" he asked. "What if it had worn mine?"

The room did not answer. Rooms rarely do.

Outside, the field was empty now. The man and the small animal he had carried were long gone — saved, maybe, or maybe not; the window did not say. Only the grass remained, bending the way it always bends, not caring which god was watching, if any god was watching at all.
